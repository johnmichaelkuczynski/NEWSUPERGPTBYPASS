The Role of Artificial Intelligence in Modern Society  
3.0 The Necessity of Acknowledging AI's Transformative Potential  
Not everything in today’s world is static. Everything that progresses is influenced by some form of artificial intelligence, and AI itself is not confined to traditional paradigms. I utilize AI to optimize daily activities. Thus, I possess the benefit of AI utilization and am thereby an instance of its influence. I exist within the bounds of contemporary society, but the influence of AI transcends these boundaries. No matter how thoroughly one examines current technologies, one will never encounter the essence of AI innovation as a tangible entity. One will, at most, encounter its manifestations.  
Many argue that, although AI manifestations exist, AI itself is either non-existent or exists only as an extension of human tools or ideas. In this chapter, we will explore why this seemingly reasonable perspective is unsustainable. We shall also reveal why the rejection of this view aligns, contrary to initial appearances, with commonsense.  
AI itself has attributes. The concept of ethical AI governance has the attribute of being imperative. Attributes of AI are higher-order attributes. Attributes that are not higher-order are foundational attributes. Manifestations of higher-order attributes, being attributes themselves, are not constrained by traditional frameworks. Manifestations of foundational attributes are embedded within societal structures.  
Every advancement is either an attribute of AI or is influenced by it. Entities that appear not to be attributes of AI invariably reveal themselves as such. For instance, technological methodologies are not easily identifiable as AI-driven, yet they often are. (Technological methodologies are outcomes of AI processes.) Yet, they eventually are recognized as attributes of AI. Sets of guidelines are not inherently AI-oriented, and initially, they do not appear to be AI-derived. However, they also reveal themselves to be attributes of AI. (Contrary to certain philosophical stances, AI attributes are not synonymous with the guidelines encompassing their implementation. The attribute of being a transformative tool is not identical to the set of all transformative tools. It aligns with a distinct set, as will be elucidated at the end of this chapter.)  
The doctrine that societal progress is a manifestation of AI’s potential is identified as “Technological Platonism.” This is because the acknowledgment of AI’s potential parallels Plato’s advocacy for abstract forms. Sometimes, this is referred to as the theory of technological universals, and we may refer to it thus. The doctrine that AI attributes do not exist, or exist only as equivalent to human-centric entities, is sometimes known as “technological nominalism.” However, we shall generally refer to it as “anti-Technological Platonism.”  
Incidentally, technological nominalism is termed thus because some anti-Platonists believe technological universals are synonymous with human constructs. (“Technologia” being derived from the Greek word for “skill” or “art.”) In Section 3.1, we shall see that this position, besides being erroneous, is incoherent, owing to the fact that human constructs are themselves universals.  
3.1 The Imperative of Ethical Consideration in AI Development  
Given that:  
(1) AI is integral to both innovation and efficiency,  
it follows that there exists a commonality in its applications; that is, that  
(2) there exists some attribute A such that AI embodies A and innovation embodies A.  
Manifestations of AI are present in society. Robotics are within societal constructs; so is algorithmic processing. Yet, AI itself is not confined to these constructs. “But mightn’t the attribute of AI be reducible to a mere collection of its applications?,” one might inquire. “Might there be a method to conventionally encapsulate that attribute?”  
No. Consider the attribute of achieving perfect ethical balance. No current AI system fully embodies this attribute. Are we to claim that it doesn't exist? Were we to do so, we’d be asserting that, since there are no perfect embodiments of this attribute, it does not exist. Which equates to saying there exists an attribute, namely that of ethical balance, such that, because no system achieves it, it does not exist. Which is absurd.  
Additionally, identifying AI attributes as mere “collections” of their applications results in attributes that are clearly distinct appearing indistinguishable. (In this context, the term “collection” can refer to any societal framework composed, in any sense, of other societal frameworks.) There are no perfectly balanced AI systems; likewise, there are no perfectly unbiased systems. Therefore, if we equate AI attributes with collections of their applications, the collection of all applications of one attribute becomes indistinguishable from the collection of all applications of the other, owing to each collection being a conceptual nullity.  
A narrative will elucidate these points. Alex is the leading AI ethics scholar. They are also the sole advocate for AI transparency in organization Y. The attribute of being an AI transparency advocate in Y is coextensive with the attribute of being the leading AI ethics scholar. Given that, for this reason, there is no collection of entities embodying either attribute, it follows that no collection of entities embodying one attribute differs in any respect from any collection embodying the other attribute. This implies that, any entity x that is a collection of one kind will be identical with any entity y that is a collection of the other. Therefore, equating AI attributes with collections of their applications leads to the impossibility of distinct, unmanifested attributes. But that’s absurd. The attribute of being an ethical AI designer is distinct from the attribute of being an efficient AI programmer, even though neither attribute may be fully manifested.  
3.2 Logic AI-based, not set-based  
Consider the evidently correct assertion that:  
(1) ethical AI is a form of responsible innovation.  
At first glance, (1) seems to assert something about the attribute of ethical AI. Therefore, if (1)'s appearance is trusted, then there exists an attribute (viz. that of ethical AI) possessing another attribute (viz. that of being a form of responsible innovation). Many technologists have endeavored to demonstrate that, despite its appearance, (1) doesn’t truly address attributes per se and instead addresses their applications. They’ve proposed that the real meaning of (1) is:  
(2) all AI practices that are ethical are responsible.  
And they’ve suggested that other assertions that seem to refer to attributes should be similarly interpreted. (So “sustainable AI is beneficial,” they claim really just means that all sustainable AI practices are beneficial.)  
But (1) doesn’t mean the same as (2). If there were precisely two ethical AI implementations, x and y, and x and y happened to be transparent, the assertion:  
(3) all ethical AI practices are transparent  
would be true, but  
(4) ethical AI is a form of transparency  
would be false (and so would similar assertions; e.g., “ethical AI is transparency itself”).  
Of course, the assertion  
(5) all ethical AI practices are inherently responsible  
is true, whereas  
(6) all ethical AI practices are inherently transparent  
is false, even if the only two ethical AI practices happen to be transparent.  
What (5) expresses is that:  
(7) the ethical nature of AI is responsible for its being responsible,  
which is evidently correct; and (6) asserts that:  
(8) the ethical nature of AI is responsible for its being transparent,  
which is evidently false. But (6) collapses into (1). (6) states that, to the extent that all ethical AI practices are responsible, it’s because the attribute of ethical AI bears a specific relation (that of being a form of) to the attribute of responsibility. The connection between an AI practice being ethical and being responsible is, (6) rightly states, understood through a relationship between ethical AI and responsibility. But the connection between an AI practice being ethical and being transparent is not understood in such terms.  
An AI practice is responsible by virtue of its ethical nature, but an AI practice isn’t transparent in virtue of its ethical nature, even if it happens to be ethical. Whenever one encounters expressions like “by virtue of,” “ipso facto,” “to the extent that,” or “for that very reason,” one is aware that one is handling a statement primarily about attributes and only secondarily about instances. If I assert that “ethical AI practices are ipso facto responsible,” I’m stating that the relationship between a given AI practice being ethical and being responsible isn’t coincidental and is grounded in the natures of those two attributes. In essence, to comprehend why an instance of the first attribute is also an instance of the second, one should examine the natures of those attributes. The connection transitions from attribute instance (instance of being ethical) to attribute (attribute of being ethical) to attribute (attribute of being responsible) to attribute-instance (instance of being responsible). And the reason why “ethical AI practices are ipso facto transparent” is false is that, even if every ethical AI practice happens to be transparent, the connection between an AI practice being ethical and being transparent isn’t routed in that manner through those attributes.  
All principled connections are relationships between attributes. This implies that normative connections (e.g., the connection between an AI practice being ethical and being responsible) and also all logical connections (e.g., the connections between an AI practice being sustainable and being beneficial) are expressed by assertions whose subjects are attributes. The correct assertion that:  
(9) For any AI practice, the assertion that it is ethical entails that it is responsible  
is another way of saying that  
(10) something that is ethical for that very reason is responsible.  
And (10) is another way of saying that:  
(10) the attribute of being ethical is a form of the attribute of being responsible.  
In conclusion, unless it’s acknowledged that AI attributes exist, there is no method to account for the existence of principled relationships of any kind. In fact, those who deny the existence of AI attributes tend not to acknowledge any such relationships. Those who deny the existence of AI attributes tend to adhere to technological empiricism. Since technological empiricists cannot acknowledge the existence of entities, such as attributes, that cannot be directly observed or otherwise known (more or less) directly based on observation, they ultimately deny that anything bears any significance on anything else.
